\section{Context}
\label{context}

The emergence of Network Science and Social Network Analysis (SNA) has provided a robust mathematical lexicon and statistical apparatus for studying complex socio-technical systems. Rather than viewing infrastructures as isolated, physical entities, Network Science allows us to abstract and model them as topological graphs where entities are nodes and their relations are edges. While originally developed for social and biological systems, this structural paradigm is increasingly vital in urban planning, transport geography, and digital humanities. It enables the quantitative study of how spatial configurations influence human mobility, municipal accessibility, and system-wide vulnerability.

This study project examines the topological structure of the public transit network of the city of Bologna, Italy. Formulated within the academic context of the Master's program in Digital Humanities and Digital Knowledge (DHDK) at the University of Bologna, this research investigates the structural transition of Bologna's mobility system. Historically reliant on a radial bus network, Bologna is currently implementing a new tram system (including the new Red and Green lines) to serve as the high-capacity backbone of its urban core. 

Our analysis models these transit layers as complex spatial networks across a structured narrative:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data and Methodology (Baseline Setup)}: Outlining GTFS and OSM data cleaning, mapping neighborhood ISTAT census demographics to stations, and constructing L-Space and P-Space topologies weighted by traffic congestion and population pressure.
    \item \textbf{Classical Baseline Transit Study}: Analyzing cognitive P-Space clustering, physical L-Space bottlenecks, macroscopic small-world indices, and resilience under random and targeted failures for the Bus Only and planned Planned Tramway.
    \item \textbf{Future Scenarios Exploration}: Proposing and testing two predictive scenarios: upgrading the circular ring road Line 32/33 into a fast tramway, and designing an optimized tramway from scratch using a multi-criteria greedy algorithm.
    \item \textbf{Commuter Shock Analysis}: Stress-testing all four networks under massive commuter injections at the central station to compare local shock absorption.
\end{itemize}

\section{Problem(s) and Motivation}
\label{problem-and-motivation}

Urban transit networks are subject to two opposing engineering and geographical pressures: the necessity for high routing efficiency and the requirement for structural resilience against localized failures. Traditional radial bus networks, which converge heavily on a few historical center-city corridors, are notoriously vulnerable to traffic congestion and localized disruptions. A single street blockage can cascade delays across dozens of lines. 

To address these challenges, we analyze the transit system across three baseline and future configurations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Bus-Only Network}: The baseline radial bus infrastructure.
    \item \textbf{Fused Bus-Tram Network}: An integrated model representing the planned Planned Tramways (Red and Green lines) where nearby bus and tram stops are merged into single nodes.
    \item \textbf{Future Tram Upgrade (Circular Line 32/33)}: A predictive scenario upgrading the circular ring road bus Line 32 (clockwise) and Line 33 (counter-clockwise) into a fast, dedicated tramway in addition to the planned tramways to evaluate its capacity to mitigate central bottleneck pressure.
    \item \textbf{Optimized Tram Network (Optimal TNDP)}: An algorithmically designed tramway network built from scratch under a fixed spatial budget.
\end{enumerate}

Our research design relies on key literature in complex networks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-World Efficiency}: Latora and Marchiori (2002) demonstrated in their study on the Boston subway that transportation networks, despite being constrained by 2D physical space (spatial planarity), can still exhibit small-world characteristics. They introduced "global efficiency" as a metric to measure how efficiently a network forwards information or passengers, which we adopt to measure travel time utility.
    \item \textbf{Robustness under Attack}: Derrible and Kennedy (2010) analyzed 33 metro systems globally, demonstrating that transit networks are often scale-free-like structures dominated by major transfer hubs. This topology exhibits high resistance to random failures but extreme fragility under targeted attacks.
\end{itemize}

The main contribution of this project is a rigorous, data-driven, and reproducible pipeline for Bologna. It models traffic congestion via the Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function, maps municipal census data, and simulates structural percolation.

\section{Dataset(s) and source(s)}
\label{datasets}

The data was compiled from multiple open municipal and open-source datasets to ensure reproducibility:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GTFS (General Transit Feed Specification)}: Obtained from Planned Tram (Trasporto Passeggeri Emilia-Romagna), the regional transport operator, representing the complete urban bus network of Bologna.
    \item \textbf{OpenStreetMap (OSM)}: Extracted via the Overpass API using a custom script. We queried railway/construction relations corresponding to the official new tram routes (Line 1/Rossa and Line 2/Verde) of Bologna.
    \item \textbf{Traffic Spire Flows}: Sourced from the Open Data portal of the Municipality of Bologna, capturing vehicle count measurements across induction loops in 2026.
    \item \textbf{Accidents Dataset}: Municipal registry of road accidents, used to calculate localized safety risk indexes within a 300-meter buffer of bus stops.
    \item \textbf{ISTAT Census Demographics (2024)}: Neighborhood-level resident populations provided by the Municipality of Bologna, grouped by statistical areas.
\end{enumerate}

We utilized the Python library \texttt{Geopandas} for spatial data processing, \texttt{Shapely} for geographic distance buffers, and \texttt{NetworkX} as the core mathematical engine to construct and analyze the L-Space network topology. The final visualizations were generated using \texttt{Matplotlib} (for static reporting graphs) and \texttt{Folium} (for interactive HTML maps). Execution environment and dependency packages were managed using the fast Python package manager \texttt{uv}.

\section{Validity and reliability}
\label{validity-and-reliability}

To guarantee the \textbf{validity} of our model (how closely it mirrors the real physical system), we developed a hybrid network-physical framework. Rather than restricting the study to a purely topological analysis, we overlaid a geographical and transportation-engineering engine. This framework implements the following core components:

\subsection{Space L vs. Space P Representation}
In transit network analysis, networks can be modeled in Space L (nodes represent physical stops, edges denote consecutive adjacent service hops) or Space P (edges connect any two stops sharing a transit line). We selected Space L because it preserves the physical layout of roads and rails. This is a prerequisite for studying infrastructural vulnerability, localized road congestion, and detour routing (Derrible and Kennedy, 2010).

\subsection{BPR Congestion Weights (Supply Friction)}
Instead of static geographical distances, edges are weighted by travel times (in seconds):
$$t_{travel} = \frac{d}{v_{effective}} + t_{dwell}$$
where $t_{dwell}$ represents the vehicle dwell time at stations. The effective speed $v_{effective}$ is modeled using the standard Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) congestion function:
$$v_{effective} = \frac{v_{base}}{1 + \alpha \left( \frac{V}{C} \right)^\beta}$$
where $v_{base}$ is the commercial base speed ($15.0\text{ km/h}$ for buses, $20.0\text{ km/h}$ for trams), $V$ is the hourly traffic volume from municipal loop detectors, $C$ is the road capacity, and $\alpha=0.15, \beta=4.0$ are standard parameters. To prevent division-by-zero errors in case of zero capacity records, $C$ is clamped to a minimum value of $1.0\text{ veh/h}$. This BPR formulation penalizes transit times along highly congested avenues, capturing peak-hour friction (Ryu et al., 2016; Pi et al., 2019). We also adjust $V$ by adding a Passenger Car Unit (PCU) equivalent representing bus dimensions to represent spatial road footprint.

\subsection{Demographics Snapping \& Baseline Passenger Pressure}
To realistically represent passenger demand, neighborhood-level resident and commuter demographic data from the ISTAT 2024 census were mapped to physical transit stops. Because GTFS stop names and census tract names do not perfectly match, we implemented a spatial snapping algorithm:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Stops with exact name matches in the census tracts are associated directly.
    \item Unmatched stops are snapped to the nearest census polygon centroid within a maximum spatial buffer radius of $250\text{ m}$.
    \item Extra-urban stops or stops beyond the buffer limits are assigned a zero baseline value.
\end{enumerate}

Running this snapping pipeline on the Bologna transit database produced the following results:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Exact matches by name}: 1,017 stops.
    \item \textbf{Spatial snaps (buffer $\le 250$m)}: 78 stops.
    \item \textbf{Unassociated/Extra-urban stops}: 188 stops.
\end{itemize}

This demographic pressure is used directly to scale the baseline dwell times ($t_{dwell}$) on edges incident to each stop. High-population nodes experience larger passenger boarding/alighting rates, inflating the station dwell times dynamically:
$$t_{dwell} = t_{base\_dwell} \cdot \left( 1 + \gamma \cdot \frac{P_{stop}}{P_{max}} \right)$$
where $t_{base\_dwell}$ is set to $10.0\text{ s}$ for buses and $15.0\text{ s}$ for trams, and $\gamma=1.0$ is the demand scaling factor. This links physical topology directly to urban demographic demand, allowing us to evaluate baseline bottlenecks under realistic pressure.

\subsection{Modeling Assumptions and Symmetries}
Several simplifying modeling assumptions were chosen for tractability:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Undirected Graph Symmetries}: The network is modeled as an undirected graph (\texttt{nx.Graph}) to allow the calculation of standard global efficiency metrics. In reality, passenger transit paths can have asymmetric behaviors.
    \item \textbf{Unified Bus Lane Congestion}: BPR congestion is applied based on overall road capacity, representing intersections where bus lanes merge with general traffic.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Targeted Centrality Attacks}: In real-world failures, traffic dynamically redistributes. We implemented a **dynamic (sequential) targeted attack** where betweenness centrality is dynamically recalculated after every node removal step (Albert et al., 2000), rather than using a static initial betweenness ranking.
\end{itemize}

To ensure \textbf{reliability} (reproducibility), all stochastic null models and percolation runs are seeded with a deterministic seed, stabilizing Watts-Strogatz indices and resilience curves across executions.

\subsubsection{ACT 1: Cognitive Topology (P-Space)}
To understand how citizens "use" the network beyond physical distances, we analyzed the P-Space, where stops are modeled as nodes and are connected by an undirected edge if they share at least one common transit line.

We evaluated this cognitive topology using two primary measures:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Louvain Community Detection}: Applying the Louvain modularity optimization algorithm to the P-Space network identifies **14 distinct cognitive communities** (Figure~\ref{fig:communities}). Geographically, these clusters represent functional transit basins (e.g., Borgo Panigale in the west, San Donato in the east, and Corticella in the north). Within each community, passengers can travel between any pair of stations using direct, single-line trips (zero transfers). Conversely, travel between different communities represents inter-basin trips that require traversing the historic center or transferring at main central hubs, highlighting the modular division of urban passenger flows.
    \item \textbf{Degree Assortativity}: We computed the Pearson correlation coefficient of degree between connected nodes ($r$). The baseline Bus Only network exhibits a slightly positive, nearly neutral assortativity of $r = 0.0120$. In P-Space, each transit route forms a fully connected subgraph (clique) where all stops share the same line, which naturally contributes a positive assortative pull since nodes within the same line clique have similar degrees. This effect is counterbalanced by major transfer hubs (such as Stazione Centrale or Piazza Malpighi) that intersect multiple lines of different sizes. When the planned tramway is integrated (Fused network), assortativity increases to $r = 0.0179$. This slight increase is driven by the introduction of long, high-capacity tram routes that act as dominant cliques, reinforcing the hub-to-hub connections along the main radial corridors.
\end{itemize}

The baseline bus network has a giant component of 1212 nodes. The planned Planned Tramway (Fused scenario) expands the component to 1283 nodes by adding 71 new peripheral or previously disconnected stops.
The Watts-Strogatz small-world index $\sigma$ is greater than 1 in both scenarios, indicating small-world properties. The collapse of Telesford's Omega ($\omega = 0.00$) is a structural property of Space L transit networks: because public transit lines are linear chains, the average node degree $\langle k \rangle \approx 2.4$. The reference regular lattice generated to match this degree has $k = 2$ (a simple cycle), which has a clustering coefficient $C_{lat} = 0.0$, making the ratio $C/C_{lat}$ in Telesford's formula undefined. Watts-Strogatz's Sigma ($\sigma$) remains a more informative indicator of small-worldness here.

The percolation curves demonstrate a classic spatial network phenomenon. Under targeted attacks, global efficiency drops much faster than the physical size of the Largest Connected Component (LCC). At a removal fraction of $5\%$ of the top betweenness centrality nodes, the LCC is still $88.83\%$ of its original size, meaning that the physical network remains largely connected. However, the global efficiency has crashed by $27.43\%$ (down to $72.57\%$ of the baseline). This is because removing the core bottlenecks (such as Farini, Marconi, and Piazza Malpighi) forces passengers to take long, circuitous detours, which increases travel times and degrades overall transit utility long before the network physically fragments. By the time $10\%$ of targeted hubs are removed, the global efficiency has dropped by $41.83\%$ (residual efficiency $58.17\%$).

In contrast, the network is highly resilient to random failures and accident-related blockages. At $5\%$ removal, random failures cause a modest $14.18\%$ drop in global efficiency, and accident-weighted removals result in a similar $14.92\%$ drop. This is because accidents and random failures tend to hit the vast majority of low-betweenness peripheral nodes, which are topologically redundant and have minimal impact on global routing.

The baseline study of Bologna's transit network reveals a crucial insight: while the planned radial Planned Tramway (Fused scenario) increases global efficiency $E_{glob}$ from $0.000526$ to $0.000554\text{ s}^{-1}$ (thanks to fast central links), it slightly increases the average travel time $L$ from $2635.49$ to $2644.83\text{ s}$. \subsection{Future Scenarios Exploration}
\label{scenarios-exploration}
To explore alternative transit strategies and optimize the planned network, we evaluate two subsequent future scenarios:

\subsubsection{Scenario A: Circular Ring Road Tramway (Lines 32 and 33)}
This scenario upgrades the circular ring road bus corridor (both Line 32 clockwise and Line 33 counter-clockwise) into a fast, dedicated tramway running along the viali di circonvallazione, in addition to Planned Tram's planned radial layout. The Circular Tram connects major suburban radial lines and bypasses the historic core entirely.

\subsubsection{Scenario B: Algorithmic Optimal Tramway Optimization (TNDP)}
To support spatial planning from an algorithmic perspective, we implemented a greenfield optimization model to design an optimal new tramway network from scratch under a length budget limit of $25\text{ km}$. This falls under the classical Transit Network Design Problem (TNDP), which is non-convex and NP-hard. The algorithm identifies three central seed hubs (\textit{Stazione Centrale}, \textit{Farini}, \textit{Piazza Cavour}) to ensure radial coverage, and greedily grows line segments by maximizing a normalized multi-criteria edge utility score:
$$U(e) = \alpha \cdot EB(e) + \beta \cdot \Delta t(e) \cdot EB(e) + \gamma \cdot \text{avg\_pop}(e)$$
where $\alpha=1.0, \beta=1.0, \gamma=1.0$. Crucially, each component is normalized using Min-Max scaling to a dimensionless scale of $[0, 1]$ across all candidate edges. The optimized spatial layout of this tramway is visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:optimized_layout}.

The optimized tramway network achieves the lowest average travel time ($2129.42\text{ s}$), saving $190.57\text{ s}$ ($8.2\%$) against the bus baseline and $207.58\text{ s}$ ($8.9\%$) against Planned Tram layout. In contrast, our greedy optimization algorithm places the $25\text{ km}$ tramway budget precisely along the high-betweenness, high-congestion, and high-density corridors of the inner urban ring and main radial paths. This demonstrates that topology-driven design models can significantly improve both commuter travel times and population coverage per unit of infrastructure built.

Under a massive commuter shock of 100,000 passengers:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The Bus Only, Planned Tram, and Optimal Tram experience extreme congestion, with travel times exploding by nearly $100\%$ (climbing from ~22-24 minutes to ~48 minutes).
    \item The Circular Tram upgrade (Lines 32/33) mitigates the congestion, maintaining an average travel time of **$39.94$ minutes** (saving **$8.73$ minutes** or $18\%$ over the planned Planned Tram layout).
    \item \textbf{Physical Analysis}: Since Stazione Centrale and Autostazione lie directly on the historic ring road, upgrading the circular ring (lines 32 and 33) into a fast tramway allows commuters to immediately disperse in both directions (clockwise and counter-clockwise) without entering the congested city core. In contrast, Planned Tram's radial lines (Red and Green) and our Optimal Tram layout route passengers through the historic center, which becomes a bottleneck under high commuter volumes. This shows that circular bypass corridors are essential to absorb passenger shocks originating at central intermodal hubs.
\end{itemize}

\section{Interpretation of the overall results}
\label{interpretation}

The analysis shows that transit networks are highly sensitive to spatial chokepoints and localized demand shocks. While radial layouts like Planned Tram's Red/Green lines and our Optimal Tram layout are effective at speeding up travel times across the municipal area, they routing passengers through the city center, making them vulnerable to localized passenger injections at major hubs. Circular bypass corridors, such as the Circular Tram, serve as vital transit loops that route traffic around the historical core. Therefore, a resilient transit strategy for Bologna must balance high-capacity radial lines with circular bypass corridors to absorb commuter shocks and prevent systemic traffic collapse.

\section{Critique (brief)}
\label{critique}

While this pipeline is highly robust, several limitations remain:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Demand Proxy}: Demographic mapping (population served) is used as a proxy for demand. However, this does not represent the actual Origin-Destination (O-D) passenger matrices. A more advanced approach would ingest real ticketing data (smart cards) to weight nodes by passenger volume.
    \item \textbf{Local Search Optimal Bias}: The greedy tramway optimizer grows lines step-by-step from seed nodes, which can be trapped in local optima. An evolutionary algorithm or simulated annealing could yield better global designs.
    \item \textbf{Uniform Headway and Transfer Cost}: The transfer friction assumes a uniform headway of 5 minutes ($t_{wait} = 150\text{ s}$) across all stops. In reality, bus lines have highly heterogeneous frequencies.
\end{enumerate}

Despite these limitations, this study successfully demonstrates the power of Network Science to evaluate urban transport changes, proving that the Bologna tramway will substantially improve city-wide mobility.
